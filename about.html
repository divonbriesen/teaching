About DI

I'm Daniel von Briesen — DI to almost everyone. I teach full time at Central Piedmont Community College in the Computer Information Systems department, and I teach as adjunct faculty at UNC Charlotte, in the Software and Information Systems area of the College of Computing and Informatics.

Between the two, my courses run from a student's first contact with a computer through database-driven web applications. I spend my weeks with people who have never written a line of code and people shipping working applications, often on the same day. My surname takes some explaining, and I wrote a page about that.

What I believe about teaching this

The Course Standards and Web Standards are what these beliefs look like once they turn into rules.

A standard you can't check isn't a standard

If I can't tell you exactly what "good" means before you start, I've no business grading you against it afterward. So I write the rules for the work down, and a script checks them. You can run that script on your own page as many times as you like, watch it go green, and submit knowing what I'll see. Grading should hold no surprises.

This runs like a job, not a seminar

I shape my courses to resemble development work rather than an academic exercise. Requirements evolve. Specifications change after you have started. Work you thought you had finished comes back with notes. That isn't a flaw in how I run a course — it's the single most accurate thing I can show you about the industry you're heading into.

So treat every assignment as a work in progress rather than a verdict. You submit, you get feedback, you improve it, you resubmit. What earns a grade here's the willingness to refine something after somebody has told you what's wrong with it, which is also what earns a living.

The habits outlast the syntax

Whichever framework dominates the job listings when you graduate, it won't be the one that dominated when you enrolled. What survives is smaller and duller: naming a thing so the name answers the question, leaving a comment that explains the decision rather than the line, validating while you build. I teach the fashionable thing because you need it, and I grade the durable thing because you'll keep it.

Struggle is the mechanism, not the obstacle

The moment where the page won't center and you can't see why isn't an interruption of learning — it's the learning. My job is to keep that struggle inside the range where you can win it, not to remove it. So I answer the question behind your question, and I'd rather hand you the search term than the snippet.

Tools are welcome; understanding is the price

I've no interest in policing which tools you open, and generative AI isn't going back in the box. The test I actually apply hasn't changed: can you explain what you turned in, and change it when I ask you to? If yes, the tool made you faster. If no, it did the course for you and you paid tuition to watch.

Everyone starts somewhere, and nobody starts in the same place

In one week I teach students meeting a file system for the first time and students deploying applications. Neither group is the "real" one. The beginner isn't behind, and the experienced student isn't done — they're working on different edges of the same craft, and both deserve a course that meets them at that edge.

Why a vicuña

Every site I build carries a Camelid Software mascot, and students name their own the same way — which is the point of the naming rule in the standards. Mine is a vicuña: a small, unfussy South American camelid that produces one of the finest fibers in the world and does it without much fanfare. It seemed like the right animal for the job.

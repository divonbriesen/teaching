FAQ

The questions students actually ask, and the reasoning behind the answers. If yours isn't here, ask it on the course discussion board and I'll add it.

Why do you make us do peer reviews?

Because reading someone else's work against a standard teaches you the standard far better than reading the standard does. You'll catch a missing alt attribute on a classmate's page a week before you stop forgetting it on your own.

It also gets you feedback faster than I can give it. Twenty students reviewing each other produce twenty rounds of correction in the time I need for one. That only works if the reviews say something — see the Peer Review Guidelines, built from an analysis of over 800 real reviews, for the difference between a review and a compliment.

Do a peer review on every assignment unless it says NPR, No Peer Review.

Why do you make us keep a journal?

The journal is where you notice what you learned, which isn't the same as doing the work. Writing "I couldn't get the footer to stay down and it turned out to be a margin on the element above it" fixes that lesson in a way that silently fixing it never does.

It also gives me a view of your thinking rather than your output. When somebody is stuck, the journal usually shows me where three weeks before the grade would.

Why do you make us keep a work log?

Because you're terrible at estimating how long things take, and so am I, and so is every developer who has ever quoted a project. The work log is where you find out. Date, start time, end time, total, where you worked, what you did — punching in and punching out of a job.

Two things fall out of it. You learn your own pace, which is the foundation of every estimate you'll ever give a client or a manager. And when you tell me an assignment took forever, we both have something specific to look at.

Why do you not want email?

Because most questions aren't private, and answering them privately wastes the answer. Ask on the course discussion board and everyone with the same question gets the answer, including the ones who didn't know they had it. You'll usually get a reply faster there too, because your classmates can answer.

Use the messaging tool in the course's learning management system when the matter is genuinely about you alone — grades, accommodations, personal circumstances. If I haven't answered and you need an answer, text me — my number is under instructor contact information in your LMS course. Say who you are, which class you're in, and what you need.

How do I ask you for a recommendation?

Write it yourself first. In a shared Google document, draft the recommendation as if you were me, then paste your resume below it, then paste a bullet list below that of anything else I should weigh — projects, context, the thing you're proud of that never showed up in a grade. Email me a link with edit access, not comment access.

Include, without me having to ask:

  • Every deadline, as the date the recipient needs it in hand
  • Your GPA
  • The specific program, scholarship, job, or transfer — not the category
  • Submission mechanics: whether a portal emails me a link, whether someone wants a PDF, whether a form demands a signature

Your draft is raw material, not the finished letter. I revise it, I cut what I can't honestly say, and I sign only what I believe. Writing it yourself speeds me up; it doesn't put words in my mouth.

Then harass me mercilessly. Start about two weeks out and keep reminding me until you can see the letter has landed. I'd much rather field five nudges than miss your deadline, and this is the one context in which I'll never find you annoying.

What if I miss a deadline?

Tell me, and keep going. Your course syllabus governs late work, and asking before the deadline gets a far better answer than asking after — but a missed deadline isn't a reason to stop, and it's never a reason to disappear.

The thing that actually sinks people isn't one late assignment. It's going quiet for three weeks because of one late assignment.

Should I wait for my grade before starting the next thing?

No. Keep moving. These courses run as a loop: do the work, submit it, get feedback, improve it, resubmit. You can revise most work, so a submission marks a checkpoint rather than a verdict, and waiting on feedback before continuing just stalls you.

I've fallen behind. How bad is it?

Recoverable, and more recoverable the sooner you say so. Assignments build on each other — each page, file, and skill supports the next one — so a gap early tends to widen rather than close on its own.

Come talk to me about a plan. I'd much rather rearrange something than watch you withdraw.

What counts as "done"?

I write down every rule I grade against, and for web work a machine checks it. Run the checker on your own page as many times as you like before you submit — see Validation for the tools and Web Standards for the rules. Nothing about your grade should be a surprise.

Can I use AI?

Usually, and it depends on the tier — the AI policy runs from ZERO-AI to ALL-AI. Check the assignment for its tier, ask when it doesn't name one yet, and read the policy before you start rather than after you finish.

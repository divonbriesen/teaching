Including Dynamic Header and Footer Components

NPR - No Peer Review.

If you aren't already including your headers and footers dynamically, now's the time to do it for all your work. The base steps are on the Common Headers/Footers Instructions page, using Paul Browne's HTMLInclude script from github.com/paul-browne/HTMLInclude. Here's what this course adds.

  • Every page includes one header and one footer and, optionally, a navbar if it isn't already in the header. Main can hold its own content, or you can include it - if you do, I suggest a folder called content for the different mains.
  • Each page's head must be unique, because the title has to include that page's title. For the full treatment, make the head a component too, and use the script's variable-passing feature to hand it the page name (see the readme in the script's repo).
  • GitHub won't validate using the referrer, so the validation links in the footer have to change to point at the calling page. Type this in rather than copying it - copying code out of a document causes trouble:
<a href="https://validator.w3.org/check?uri=" id="validation_link_html">HTML validation</a>
<a href="https://jigsaw.w3.org/css-validator/validator?uri=" id="validation_link_css">CSS validation</a>
<script>
document.getElementById("validation_link_html").setAttribute("href",
  "https://validator.w3.org/check?uri=" + location.href);
document.getElementById("validation_link_css").setAttribute("href",
  "https://jigsaw.w3.org/css-validator/validator?uri=" + location.href);
</script>

This isn't the most efficient way to do it. For a challenge, learn to read the contents in with your own script, or from a JSON file, and talk it over on the forum.

M09 - Describe Your Project

Say what you intend to build, in enough detail that someone else could tell whether you finished it. Everything from here to M16 is that one project.

Describe Your Project

You've finished a MERN tutorial and a good run of freeCodeCamp work with HTML, CSS, JavaScript, and React. Now it's time to think about an online project. There are two big parts: building the app - modifying the one from the tutorial, or one of your own, or starting from scratch - and figuring out where and how to host it.

The Project
  • Something you care about. Discuss on the forum if you need ideas.
  • It needs data persistence in MongoDB with the equivalent of a many-to-many relationship - one person can own many cars, and one car can have more than one owner, which a traditional relational database would handle with a join table.
  • It needs authentication - some kind of login.
  • Sample data (AI is fine): at least a dozen values prepopulated. We should be able to load these at first, and eventually do CRUD on them and anything you add.
The Plan and the Host

Begin a project plan and research where you'll host. You may choose a paid account for your own benefit - you only need to pay for one month for this, and you can probably do it for about $10. There are free sites, and Heroku has been used in the past, but those can be harder.

  • Start by getting a very primitive app going that represents one of your pages, like your introduction or contract, and make sure it runs on your computer through node.
  • Then research hosting and put that simple app on the site you host with. Submit the link, and make sure the page loads. The next step is having your whole site load, so you can begin that as you go.
  • Make a branch for this in your GitHub work. GitHub Pages may not work with the MERN work, and you don't want to break your older pages.
  • Add a link in the footer of your common menu that leads here, and a link in your header nav that leads to these assignments. We should be able to click through to your github.io pages and follow links to your hosted work.

Describe the project here in as much detail as you're comfortable with, and where you plan to host it. Your data may live somewhere different.

Cookies: Make, Bake, and Take

Read up on cookies and how to create, delete, and view them with JavaScript. Wherever it's appropriate you may use local storage in place of cookies. Two of the most common uses:

  1. Tracking whether you're logged in, so every other page or part of the app can confirm it. These are session variables, because they track your browsing session.
  2. Tracking whether this person (presumably) has been here before, when, with what browser, and so on. A catalog site can follow what you were looking at even if you never logged in, then offer specials next time. This is getting more complex - and more intrusive - as platforms swap data around. This exercise teaches the basics with an automatic cookie and a manual cookie.
Build
  • Make a copy of your introduction form assignment.
  • Above the form, show two sets of non-editable information: browser and version, session info, timestamp, and IP address.
  • Create a cookie that saves that information plus everything in the form.
  • On load, populate the form from the saved values. The first set shows the current values; the second pulls the last values from the cookie.
  • Collect the information with JavaScript when the page loads and display it. When the user saves, save it along with the form data. Think of these as extra form fields.
  • Retrieve the cookies, if they're there, and display each item in a formatted way, like browser: … || timestamp: ….
  • Delete one cookie, the other, or both. Add a button to purge the cookie.
To Test
  • The first time you visit, it shows that there's no cookie information.
  • Go back, and it shows the automatic cookie's information.
  • Submit the form, and it refreshes on save and shows that.
  • Leave and come back, and it shows both.
  • Delete one and go back, and it shows only the other - but if you deleted the automatic one, it should regenerate by itself, so how will that work?

It may look complex, but break it into modules and methods and think through the logic. Use if and else properly so that if you have a result you show it, and if you don't you don't try. Ask or discuss - don't be shy. Link it in your table of contents as usual, and do your classmates a favor: really test theirs before I get to it.

Using Sprites

A CSS sprite is one image file holding many small images, shown a piece at a time. Start by reading about them - CSS Tricks and Level Up Tutorials - and try Sprite Cow, which writes the positioning CSS for you. Find a few more articles and consider sharing them on the discussion forum.

Then create or find a set of sprites that fits your site's theme and build a basic page that uses them. It must show a single spritesheet with multiple sprites, used in different places or in different ways. As always, the page carries your standard linked CSS and passes the validators.

Everything HTML

Build a single page, themed to your site, that uses and, where it can, demonstrates every HTML5 tag. The list of tags is online in several places (W3Schools has one), and with a little creativity you can get it into a format where much of the work is done for you.

Part I: The Menu
  • Make a new page - "my everything HTML page" will do - with your standard branding and validators. As on every page, put a heading with the assignment's title under your normal header.
  • At the top, list every HTML5 tag as a menu, alphabetically, each one a link: <!-- --> || <!DOCTYPE> || <a> || <abbr> || <address> and so on. It can be a horizontal line or a vertical list; if it's vertical, the content should appear to its right, which takes a more dynamic layout.
  • Most of these links point to somewhere lower on the same page, so review internal anchors - they're how this assignment works. This page has the pattern: each section has an anchor, and a link back to the top.
Part II: The Demonstrations

Keep the menu alphabetical. Several tags can share one destination - <table>, <tr>, <th>, and <td> can all lead to one themed example table. Each link gets a "back to menu" or "back to top" link.

Part III: Finish

Every tag in the menu leads to something that shows what it does. Where a tag can't be demonstrated - a comment, the doctype - link to a statement that says the tag is there and what it does. The page is themed, validates, and lists them all.

Everything HTML, From Data (aka "Dynamize")

Do Everything HTML first. This one turns it into a page that builds itself from data - "dynamizing" it. Your Everything HTML page is still manual: if it were a list of people in a company, you'd be editing it every time somebody joined, left, or changed their name. Here the information comes from data instead, so changing it means changing one line of a file.

Each tag has a handful of data points, all of them text: its name, its description, its relationship to HTML5 (not supported, replaced by…), your themed example, and anything else you'd add.

  • Put the data in a delimited text file, one record per line, five values each. Use a delimiter like || - commas and ampersands cause trouble here - and make it obvious, so debugging is easy.
  • Your code opens and reads the file, and knows when to stop (or not to start): open if it's there, read while there are values left.
  • Separate the records, and put the values into a two-dimensional array - one dimension is the record, the other its values. Loading everything once is usually simplest, after which you only read from the array.
  • Use the first value to generate the menu, extract the one the user chose, format it, and output it to the page.

Want a stretch? Once it works, move the records into MongoDB and read them from there.

Console Play

Using any app that shows the JavaScript console - your browser's developer tools, or JSFiddle - write at least 50 expressions that show different bits of code at play, using material from your reading. If you did something like this in WEB115, this time work on more complex or tricky examples. It's different from the Intermediate JavaScript tutorial: there you follow lessons, here you write the experiments yourself.

  • Avoid more than a few alerts. For output use console.log or document.write. For input use prompt, or an HTML button and textfield if you want to be fancy.
  • Show some math, some string manipulation, some variable declarations, some logic and flow control, and try to create functions.
  • Try to break code and then fix it. Don't be shy about experimenting.
  • A simple route is to cycle through examples in your reading, but customize them - that isn't copying if you make it yours.

Paste the code and results right into your post, or link to where they are. Don't attach a text file. Reply to two classmates, saying what they missed or what needs improving - or that it's perfect, if it is.

A One-Div Graphic

Look at this CodePen and figure out what's going on. Then make your own: find a logo or graphic and see how well you can recreate it with CSS in a single div.

  • Include a copy of the original (unless you made it from scratch), a link to your work on your site, and a screenshot of what it looks like to you, at its best, in your browser. These don't render reliably everywhere, so the screenshot shows what you see and the link shows what we see.
  • This isn't trivial - it's why people say "wow" when they see one online - so give yourself time. You'll do better recreating an existing logo than inventing one.
  • Don't overcomplicate it. A complex logo makes this much harder than it has to be. The point is to get a feel for how this works, then move on.
  • Put it on a new page, linked from your site menu.

Node Hello World

Pairs well with Learn Node.js. Go to nodebeginner.org and get Node running on your system by reaching the Hello World part - customized to your site's theme, of course.

Then tweak the code to be cleaner and easier to understand. Comment it line by line, simplify it as much as you can, and avoid repeating code or function calls. Add the rest of an HTML document around the output - the head, the title, and so on - so it's valid.

Make a page for this assignment, linked from your site, that shows your Hello World greeting running locally on a node server as a screenshot, then your code, then the output of your modified, branded home page shown right in the page - as formatted text and images, not links.

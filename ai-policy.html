AI Policy

Generative AI is neither banned nor mandatory in my courses. It depends entirely on what a particular assignment is trying to teach you, so every assignment carries a level from 1 to 4. Read the level before you start, not after you finish.

Each assignment names its level. When an assignment says nothing, ask me — don't assume the most permissive reading, and don't assume the most restrictive one either.

Level 1 — No AI whatsoever

Don't use generative AI at any point on this work. Not for the outline, not for the code, not for the grammar pass, not to explain the error message. When I set an assignment at level 1, I want to see what you can do unaided, because either the struggle is the lesson or I need an honest reading of where you actually are.

Using AI here's an academic integrity violation, and I handle it as one. If you're unsure whether a tool counts — an autocomplete, a grammar checker, a search result with an AI summary at the top — ask first.

Level 2 — Brainstorming only

Use AI to get unstuck at the start. Generate ideas, explore approaches, argue with it about which direction to take, ask it what you might be missing. Then close it and build the thing yourself.

The line falls at production: the words, the code, and the design you submit come from you. AI helped you decide what to make; it doesn't get to make it.

Level 3 — Your call

Use it, or don't. I trust you to judge where it helps your learning and where it short-circuits it, and part of what you practice at this level is making that judgment deliberately rather than by habit.

Expect AI to be genuinely useful here. Also expect that leaning on it for something you haven't learned yet will cost you on the next assignment, which is the real feedback loop.

Level 4 — AI expected

I fully expect you to use AI on this work, and I've scoped the assignment assuming you'll. Working effectively with these tools is part of the skill I'm teaching, so submitting something you laboriously hand-made isn't extra credit.

What I grade at this level is your judgment: the quality of what you asked for, whether you caught what the model got wrong, and what you changed about its output and why. A clean, unedited, confidently incorrect answer scores badly.

Disclosure, at every level above 1

Whenever you use AI, say so. Name the tool and describe what you used it for in a sentence or two — brainstorming, debugging, drafting a specific section. Put it with the submission, not buried in a comment.

Disclosure costs you nothing. Hiding it costs you the assignment, and an undisclosed use at a level that would have permitted it's a far worse outcome than the use itself.

What doesn't change at any level

  • You own what you submit, including its errors. "The AI said so" is not a defense — the hallucinated citation, the API that doesn't exist, and the code that silently does the wrong thing are all yours once you turn them in.
  • You must be able to explain your submission and change it on request. That test decides more of my grading than any detector would.
  • Don't paste anything into a tool that you wouldn't post publicly: your classmates' work, anything with personal information in it, or material a client or employer gave you in confidence.
  • Where your institution's academic integrity policy is stricter than this page, it wins. See also Course Standards.

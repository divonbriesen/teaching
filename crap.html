CRAP

Four principles that carry most of the weight in visual design, and the fastest way for someone who does not consider themselves a designer to stop producing pages that feel wrong without knowing why.

These come from Robin Williams, The Non-Designer's Design Book: Design and Typographic Principles for the Visual Novice (Peachpit Press, 1994). The book is short, cheap, and worth owning.

Contrast

If two things are not the same, make them clearly different. Timid differences read as mistakes — a heading one point larger than the body text looks like an error rather than a decision. Push it until nobody could mistake it for an accident.

On your pages: heading sizes, weight against regular text, a link colour nobody could confuse with body copy, and a foreground that stays readable when someone turns their brightness down on a bus.

Repetition

Repeat something — a colour, a shape, a typeface, a spacing rhythm — across the whole piece. Repetition is what makes ten separate pages feel like one site rather than ten pages that happen to link to each other.

On your pages: the same header and footer everywhere, navigation in the same place every time, brand colours and type used the same way throughout. This is the principle the Course Site Standards lean on hardest.

Alignment

Nothing goes anywhere arbitrarily. Every element sits in a visual relationship with something else on the page. Pick an edge and commit to it — mixing centred and left-aligned text in one block is the single most common thing that makes student work look amateur.

On your pages: a consistent left edge for text, headings lining up with the content they head, form labels sharing an alignment, and centring used deliberately rather than as a default.

Proximity

Group related things together, and put space between groups. Closeness tells a reader what belongs with what before they read a word. A caption touching its image is one idea; a caption floating equidistant between two images belongs to neither.

On your pages: a heading nearer the text it introduces than the section above it, list items tighter to each other than to surrounding paragraphs, and whitespace used as a tool rather than left over.

About the name

Williams presents them in the order Proximity, Alignment, Repetition, Contrast — PARC, the sequence the book teaches and the one that builds most naturally. CRAP is the rearrangement everybody remembers, which is exactly why I use it. The four principles stay the same either way.

Why naming them matters

Williams opens with what she calls the Joshua tree principle: once somebody tells you the name of a thing, you start seeing it everywhere. You have reacted to contrast and alignment your whole life without having words for them. Having the words lets you fix a page instead of merely disliking it.

The Commandments ask you to design on purpose with these four. The Web Standards check what a machine can; these are the part you have to see for yourself.
